A usable thermal printer in Nepal starts around Rs 4,000–6,000 for a small 58 mm Bluetooth unit, Rs 7,000–12,000 for an 80 mm counter printer with USB, and Rs 13,000–20,000 for an 80 mm LAN printer meant to sit in a kitchen. Those are the bands that matter; the KOT printer price in Nepal you actually pay depends more on which connection you pick than on the brand.

Prices are indicative for 2026 from Kathmandu dealers and online listings. Ask for a quote with the cable, the first paper rolls and the warranty written on it — those three turn a cheap printer into an average-priced one.

For a restaurant issuing VAT bills, 80 mm is the sensible default. A Nepali bill carries the food lines, service charge, taxable value and VAT, and a 32-character line turns that into a mess.

2. Which connection, and why it decides the KOT printer price in Nepal

  • USB (Rs 7,000–12,000). Cheapest reliable option. Fine at the counter where the billing machine sits. Useless on the other side of a wall.
  • LAN / Ethernet (Rs 13,000–20,000). The right answer for a kitchen printer. It takes a fixed IP, sits on your router, and any till in the restaurant can send it a ticket. Costs more up front and saves you the weekly "the kitchen printer stopped" problem.
  • Bluetooth (Rs 4,000–9,000). Good for a phone-based till, a cart or a delivery bag. Pairing drops, and range through a kitchen wall is optimistic.
  • Wi-Fi (Rs 14,000–22,000). Convenient until the Wi-Fi is busy. In a kitchen with steam, metal and a fridge compressor, a cable wins.

If you are buying two printers — one for bills at the counter, one for KOT in the kitchen — the usual pairing is USB at the counter and LAN in the kitchen.

3. What a kitchen printer needs that a counter printer does not

  1. A buzzer. A ticket nobody hears is a ticket nobody cooks.
  2. Auto-cutter. Tearing by hand with wet gloves, forty times a service, gets old.
  3. Heat tolerance. Mount it away from the stove line and out of the steam path, or the paper curls and the head ages fast.
  4. A fixed IP. On DHCP the address changes after a power cut and the printer "disappears". Set it once, write it on a sticker on the printer.

4. The three cheap buys that cost more

  • A no-name printer with no local service. When the head goes at month nine, there is nobody to take it to. Buy from a dealer who will.
  • Bluetooth for the kitchen. It works on the demo. It does not work across a kitchen at 8pm.
  • Thin paper rolls. Cheap rolls jam, fade within weeks and wear the head. On a VAT bill you are legally meant to keep, print that fades is a real problem.

5. Running cost nobody budgets

Paper. An 80 mm roll is commonly Rs 35–60 depending on quality and quantity. A restaurant printing bills and KOTs can go through 60–120 rolls a month, which is Rs 2,500–7,000 — more than the monthly fee of most billing software. Buying rolls by the box, not by the piece, is the easiest saving in this list.

Common questions

Do I need a separate KOT printer? If the kitchen is more than a few steps from the counter, yes. Before that, printing both on one counter printer and carrying the slip is fine.

Can I use one printer for bills and KOTs? Yes, and many small places do. The limit is traffic: during a rush, bills and tickets queue behind each other.

Is a kitchen screen cheaper than a printer? Over a year, often yes, because there is no paper. It needs a screen, a stand and a steady connection, and some kitchens simply prefer paper.

What about printing from a phone? A Bluetooth 58 mm printer and a phone is the cheapest working setup there is, and it is how a lot of carts and small shops bill. Move to 80 mm when the bill gets longer than the paper.

How long does a thermal printer last? Three to five years in a counter, less in a hot kitchen. The print head is the part that wears, and paper quality decides how fast.
