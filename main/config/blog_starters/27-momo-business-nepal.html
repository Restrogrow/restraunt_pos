A momo business is profitable in Nepal more often than most food businesses, because the food cost per plate is low, the equipment is cheap and the demand is year-round. It is also easy to run at a loss, for one reason: the margin per plate is small in rupees, so everything depends on how many plates go out and how little gets thrown away.

The numbers below are illustrative and built from typical Kathmandu costs in 2026. Your supplier prices, rent and portion size will move them. Use the method, not the figures.

1. What one plate actually costs

A 10-piece plate of buff momo, made in-house:

ItemCost per plate
Filling (buff mince, onion, spices)Rs 28
Flour and wrapperRs 7
Achar, soup, garnishRs 6
Gas and electricity shareRs 5
Packaging (for takeaway)Rs 8
Food and direct costRs 46–54

At Rs 140 a plate dine-in, that is a gross margin of roughly Rs 90, or 64%. Good — but gross margin is not profit. Rent, salaries and the rest come out of it.

2. A worked month for a small shop

Eight seats, two staff plus the owner cooking, Rs 30,000 rent, 120 plates a day across dine-in and takeaway, average bill Rs 180 once drinks and a second plate are counted.

That is the good case, and it assumes 120 plates a day every day. At 60 plates a day the same shop makes roughly Rs 90,000 — still a living, but the rent and salaries have not changed, and one slow month takes most of it. Footfall is the whole business.

3. Four ways people run a momo business

  • Shop with seating. Highest sales per customer, highest rent. Needs a street people already walk down.
  • Takeaway counter or cart. Lowest setup, fastest to test an area. Weather and municipal rules decide a lot.
  • Frozen momo supply. You make, pack and sell to shops and households. Lower price per piece, far higher volume, and you need a freezer, labelling and a food licence that covers production.
  • Cloud kitchen on delivery apps. No dining room, so rent is low — but Pathao Food takes about 18% and Foodmandu 20–22% of each order, as The Record has reported. At those rates a Rs 180 plate loses Rs 32–40 before the mince is paid for.

4. Where a momo business loses money

  1. Portion drift. The filling scoop creeps up by five grams and nobody notices. Over a month that is thousands of rupees. Weigh the filling for a week and fix the scoop.
  2. Waste at close. Steamed momo does not keep. Prepare in batches against the hour, not the day.
  3. Buff price swings. Mince is the biggest line and it moves. Check your cost per plate monthly, not yearly.
  4. Free achar and soup. Small per plate, large per month. Cost it, then decide to give it away on purpose rather than by accident.
  5. No idea what sold. Owners who bill every plate know that jhol momo outsells steam two to one after 6pm, and they cook to that. Owners who do not are guessing with their own money.

5. What it costs to start

A small momo shop is among the cheapest restaurants to open: a steamer line, a prep table, a fridge, simple seating and signage usually come to Rs 8–10 lakh including the deposit, with Rs 2–3 lakh of working capital behind it. The full line-by-line version is in the cost to open a restaurant in Nepal, and the licences are the same as any other food business — see restaurant registration in Nepal.

6. The one habit that separates the two outcomes

Bill every plate, from day one, on something that keeps the record. Not for the tax office — for you. Within a month you know your busiest two hours, your best-selling variant, your real average bill and your true food cost percentage. Every decision above, from the scoop size to whether delivery apps are worth it, depends on numbers you only have if the till collected them. A phone and a thermal printer are enough to start; restaurant POS software in Nepal covers what it needs to handle, including VAT and Nepali bill numbering.

Common questions

How much profit does a momo shop make in a month? A busy small shop can clear Rs 1.5–3 lakh before the owner's own wage; a quiet one makes a modest living. Plates per day is the variable that matters.

Is frozen momo more profitable than a shop? Per piece, less. In total, often more, because volume is not limited by how many people walk past. It needs more licensing and a cold chain.

What is a good food cost percentage? Around 30–35% of sales for momo. Above 40%, check your portions and your buying before you blame the rent.

Should I join delivery apps? Early on, yes, for visibility. Once you have regulars, give them a direct way to order so the commission is not charged on people who were coming to you anyway.
