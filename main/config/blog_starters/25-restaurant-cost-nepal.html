The cost to open a restaurant in Nepal usually lands between Rs 5 lakh for a small momo shop and Rs 40 lakh or more for a fine-dining place, with most mid-size restaurants in Kathmandu starting somewhere around Rs 15 to 30 lakh. The spread is that wide because two numbers dominate everything else: the deposit your landlord asks for, and how much kitchen you have to build from scratch.

The figures below are illustrative ranges put together from Nepali advisory and training sources in 2026. Prices move, and Kathmandu is dearer than everywhere else. Use them to build your own list, not as a quote.

2. The line most people leave out

Working capital. A new restaurant does not break even in month one, and you still owe rent, salaries and the vegetable supplier. Budget three to six months of running cost on top of the setup figure above. For a mid-size place paying Rs 1,00,000 rent and Rs 2,00,000 in salaries, that is another Rs 9–18 lakh sitting in the bank doing nothing — and it is the difference between riding out a slow monsoon and closing in month seven.

A Kathmandu newspaper reported in 2026 that roughly half of new restaurants in the valley shut within twelve months. Almost none of them close because the food was bad. They close because month four arrived and there was nothing left to pay with.

3. Three worked examples

A momo shop, 8 seats, side street

Deposit Rs 2 lakh, rent Rs 30,000, a steamer-led kitchen at Rs 3 lakh, simple interior Rs 2 lakh, licences Rs 25,000, a phone-based till and thermal printer Rs 25,000, stock Rs 50,000, signage Rs 50,000. Setup about Rs 8.5 lakh, plus Rs 2–3 lakh working capital. One owner-cook, two helpers.

A 30-seat cafe, good street

Deposit Rs 5 lakh, rent Rs 60,000, kitchen and coffee machine Rs 6 lakh, interior Rs 5 lakh, licences Rs 50,000, billing and hardware Rs 60,000, stock Rs 1 lakh, launch Rs 1 lakh. Setup about Rs 19 lakh, plus Rs 5–7 lakh working capital.

A 60-seat restaurant with a bar

Deposit Rs 12 lakh, rent Rs 1.5 lakh, kitchen Rs 9 lakh, interior Rs 10 lakh, licences and liquor permissions Rs 1 lakh, billing with KOT and bar tickets Rs 1.2 lakh, stock Rs 3 lakh, launch Rs 2 lakh. Setup about Rs 38 lakh, plus Rs 12–18 lakh working capital.

4. What you can cut, and what you should not

  • Cut: imported furniture, a large kitchen for a small menu, heavy civil work in a rented space you may leave, a second fridge "for later".
  • Cut: paying for a year of software before you have served a single customer. Pay monthly until you know the place works.
  • Do not cut: the exhaust and the wiring. Both are expensive to fix after the kitchen is built, and both are what fire-safety inspections look at.
  • Do not cut: working capital. Every other line can be made smaller. This one cannot.
  • Do not cut: proper billing from day one. A restaurant that cannot tell you its real daily sales cannot tell you whether it is dying until it already has.

5. The recurring costs that decide whether it survives

Setup is a one-off. What kills margins is monthly: rent, salaries, gas and electricity, raw material, and the commission on delivery apps. Pathao Food takes about 18% and Foodmandu 20–22% of each order, as reported by The Record. On a Rs 800 order that is Rs 145–175 gone before you have paid for the food. Delivery apps are worth it for visibility while you are unknown; they are a problem once your regulars use them out of habit. Moving even a third of those regulars to direct orders through your own site or WhatsApp is usually worth more than any cost you cut in the kitchen — zero commission ordering walks through how.

6. Before you sign the lease

  1. Count the footfall outside the shutter yourself, at lunch and at 8pm, on a weekday and a Saturday.
  2. Ask what the previous tenant was and why they left.
  3. Check the water supply and the three-phase line before you fall in love with the place.
  4. Get the licence list in writing — registration, PAN, ward, food licence — and price it. Restaurant registration in Nepal lists the steps.
  5. Write the menu before you buy the kitchen, not after. The menu decides the equipment.

Common questions

Can I open a small restaurant with Rs 5 lakh? A momo or chiya shop with a few seats, yes, if the deposit is small and you do the cooking. A 30-seat cafe at that budget means skipping working capital, which is the usual reason for closing.

Is a franchise cheaper? No, but the risk profile is different. You pay a fee and follow their system; what you buy is a known menu and some demand on day one.

How long until it breaks even? Owners commonly plan for six to twelve months. Plan for the longer end and be pleased if it comes sooner.
